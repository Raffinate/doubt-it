How to play

What is Doubt It?

Doubt It is the classic bluffing card game — play cards face down claiming they're all one type, or call out the last player for lying — with a high-stakes twist: whoever's wrong ends up spinning a revolver. A match is an elimination duel that can span several rounds of cards, with your revolver's chambers carrying over between them.

The deck

20 cards, but only two things ever matter: whether a card is Safe (8 in the deck) or a Liar card (12 in the deck). Each player is dealt 5 fresh cards every round, shown only as a safe/liar count — never the specific cards.

How a round plays

  1. At the start of a match, both players' revolvers are loaded full (6 chambers), public and unchanging until someone actually spins.
  2. Each round, both players are dealt 5 fresh cards (a safe/liar count).
  3. Who opens: round 1 of a match is a coin flip. After that, whoever spun and survived the previous round's call sits out the opening move — the other player opens the next round.
  4. On your turn, play 1-3 cards face down — claimed as all Safe — or call Doubt on the last play.
  5. A play must be all-safe or all-liar; mixing is never useful and isn't offered.
  6. The very first play of a round can't be challenged — nothing's been played yet.
  7. If your opponent just emptied their hand, calling Doubt is your only legal move.

Resolution

Calling Doubt reveals the challenged play. If it contained any Liar card, the player who played it lied and must spin; otherwise the caller was wrong and spins instead. That player ("the spinner") spins the revolver alone, once, at their own current chambers — the other player's chambers are untouched. Die, and the match is over. Survive, and your own chambers drop by one, then the match continues: a brand new round is dealt, with chambers exactly as they now stand. This repeats — only the spinner's side ever takes damage in any given round — until an eventual solo spin ends the match.

Note: being correct about a call and surviving that round's spin are two different things, tracked separately. A correct call can still end badly if your own chambers are unlucky — the same way winning an all-in in poker doesn't mean the shove was the right price.
